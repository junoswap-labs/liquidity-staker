Executive summary

Permissionless factory for single-pool staking on KUB Chain. Anyone calls deploy to create a StakingRewards pool, choosing the staking token, the reward token, the budget, the start time, the epoch length, a per-stake lock and a total staking cap, and funding the rewards up front; the same transaction starts epoch 1. The creator funds later epochs with startEpoch, retires the pool with close, and reclaims only the emissions that accrued while the pool was empty. Reward accounting is the Synthetix accumulator (rewardPerTokenStored / userRewardPerTokenPaid), with per-deposit lock lots and an ERC-20/KAP-20 allowance fallback.

One Critical finding. The accumulator step divides by the total stake and discards the remainder, and getRewardFor(address) is permissionless — so an attacker chooses how often the settlement happens. Settling once per block makes the increment round to zero every time on a low-decimal reward token such as KUSDT, while still consuming the elapsed seconds. A 1 000 KUSDT budget over a 1 000 000-unit 18-decimal LP pool pays out 999 of its 1 000 tokens if left alone and 0 under this attack, for the price of gas (C-01). This was rated High on the first pass, on the wrong assumption that the loss tracks organic activity; it was escalated on re-review once the permissionless settlement path was traced.

Two High findings remain from the same design decision — the factory accepts arbitrary tokens from anyone. A fee-on-transfer staking token silently desynchronised _totalSupply and left the last stakers unable to withdraw (H-01, fixed by crediting the measured balance delta); and any KAP-20 token's committee can move a pool's balance out or freeze every transfer out of it (H-03, inherent — mitigated by an optional owner allowlist that ships off, and by front-end disclosure).

A follow-up review of the withdrawal path specifically - asking whether withdraw can ever revert, rather than assuming - found H-04: the reward accumulator had no upper bound, and overflowing it made updateReward revert on every path including withdrawal, freezing stakers' principal permanently. Reproduced, then closed by bounding the epoch budget at configuration time. The same review confirmed the useful half of that property: withdraw touches only the staking token, so a broken reward token cannot hold principal hostage (I-05).

Remediation status: C-01, H-01, M-01, M-02, M-03, L-01, L-02, L-03, I-01, I-03 and I-04 are fixed and carry regression tests in test/Regressions.spec.ts; H-03 and I-01 are acknowledged with the mitigations described in each. Fixing the factory also exposed an EIP-170 problem: the factory embeds the pool's initcode, and at optimizer_runs = 999999 its runtime reached 25 719 bytes, over the 24 576-byte limit — it was undeployable. The optimizer now runs at 1 000 (factory 21 249 bytes, pool 13 366).

A reuse pass on re-review merged three duplicated paths that were costing users gas - a double accumulator settle on every state change, a double settle plus double lot walk in exit(), and two copies of the withdrawal tail - and a new read-only StakingRewardsLens collapses a pool page from twenty-odd eth_calls to one (I-01, I-04).

The contract is well documented, the arithmetic is deliberate rather than accidental, and the test suite covers the reward maths across 0/2/6/8/18-decimal tokens. The residual gaps are at the boundary — which tokens are allowed in — not in the core accounting.

Trust assumptions & out of scope

  • Not audited: OpenZeppelin Contracts 4.9.6 (Ownable, SafeERC20, ReentrancyGuard, Math), solc 0.8.19, and the KUB Chain client itself. All are assumed to behave as documented.
  • Not audited: the staking and reward tokens. Pool creation is permissionless, so every pool inherits the full trust surface of whatever tokens its creator chose — including any KAP-20 committee, blocklist, pause switch or KYC gate. H-03 scopes this.
  • Not audited: the front end. Several findings (M-01, H-02) are only safe if the UI reads live pool state rather than the factory's poolInfo index, and warns on parameter combinations that truncate.
  • The factory owner is assumed to be a multisig or timelock. A single EOA raises M-03 to High.
  • A pool creator is trusted only with respect to their own capital: they can retire the pool and reclaim unearnable emissions, and cannot reach staked principal or rewards a staker has already earned. This was verified, not assumed.
  • StakingRewards is analysed as deployed by the factory. Deploying it standalone with an arbitrary rewardsDistribution address widens the surface (that address controls the schedule and could notify a reward larger than was actually funded, since the balance check counts staked principal when both tokens are the same) and is out of scope.
  • The working tree at the time of review is not committed. Findings reference the files as read, not as published at c580963.
  • Advisory only, scoped to the code and state described. Not a warranty; residual risk stays with the team.

Detailed findings

Critical

C-01 · Reward accrual truncates to zero on low-decimal reward tokens, and any caller can force the truncation every block until the whole budget is stranded

Fixed

StakingRewards.sol:156-166 (`rewardPerToken`), StakingRewards.sol:419-450 (`notifyRewardAmount`), StakingRewards.sol:497-514 (`updateReward`)

Description

rewardPerToken() advances the accumulator by floor((applicable - last) * rewardRate / supply) and discards the remainder. rewardRate is scaled by 1e18, which fixes the *rate* division, but the division by totalStaked is unscaled — so every settlement throws away up to one whole unit of rewardPerToken, worth totalStaked / 1e18 reward base-units across the pool.

updateReward runs on every stake, withdraw, getReward, getRewardFor, exit and notifyRewardAmount, so the loss scales with how busy the pool is, not with elapsed time. A pool nobody touches until the epoch ends loses nothing; a pool touched a thousand times loses a thousand increments.

The magnitude depends entirely on the reward token's decimals. Concretely, with KUSDT (6 decimals) as the reward token, a 1 000 KUSDT budget (1e9 base units) over 30 days, and 1 000 000 units of an 18-decimal LP staked (1e24):

  • rewardRate = 1e9 * 1e18 / 2 592 000 = 3.858e20
  • one second of accrual: floor(1 * 3.858e20 / 1e24) = 0
  • each settlement discards up to 1e24 / 1e18 = 1e6 base units = 1.0 KUSDT

At 1 000 interactions over the epoch the entire 1 000 KUSDT budget can be consumed by truncation while earned() reports approximately zero for everyone.

The stranded amount is not recoverable. unallocatedRewards only accrues while _totalSupply == 0, and this loss happens precisely when the pool *is* staked, so recoverUnallocatedRewards returns nothing for it. The project's own test suite encodes the mechanism — maxDust(totalStaked) = totalStaked / 1e18 + 10 — and observes 880 wei of loss on an 18-decimal token, which is the same formula at a harmless scale.

Upstream Synthetix has the identical final division, so this is inherited rather than introduced. It matters far more here because KUSDT, the flagship KUB stablecoin, has 6 decimals, and because the README claims the precision problem is fixed.

Escalated to Critical on re-review. The first pass rated this High on the assumption that the loss tracks organic pool activity. It does not: getRewardFor(address) is permissionless, so *the attacker chooses how often the accumulator settles*. Settling once per block forces the increment to be computed over the smallest possible Δt, which is exactly when floor((Δt * rewardRate) / supply) is most likely to be zero — and a zero increment advances lastUpdateTime anyway, so the elapsed second is consumed and its emissions are gone.

Carrying the numbers through for a 1 000 KUSDT (1e9 base units) budget over 30 days with 1 000 000 units of an 18-decimal LP staked (1e24), at the audited PRECISION = 1e18:

``` rewardRate = 385802469135802469135 settle every 1s -> increment = 0 settle every 5s -> increment = 0 settle every 60s -> increment = 0 settle every 3600s -> increment = 1 settle every 2592000s -> increment = 999

settled every block (518 400 settlements): rewardPerToken total = 0 settled once at the end: rewardPerToken total = 999 ```

So the same pool pays out 999 000 000 of its 1 000 000 000 base units if left alone, and 0 if a stranger spends one transaction per block on it. That is an unprivileged, cheap, fully deterministic griefing attack on the pool's entire budget, not a rounding nuisance — hence Critical. The attack needs no stake, no approval and no timing; on a cheap-gas chain such as KUB the cost is negligible against the budget destroyed.

Vulnerable code
uint256 public constant PRECISION = 1e18; function rewardPerToken() public view returns (uint256) {    uint256 supply = _totalSupply;    ...    unchecked {        return rewardPerTokenStored + ((applicable - last) * rewardRate) / supply;    }} function notifyRewardAmount(uint256 reward, ...) external {    uint256 rate = (reward * 1e18) / _rewardsDuration;}
Impact

An attacker with no stake and no privileges destroys the pool's entire reward budget for the price of gas, by calling getRewardFor once per block. The rewards are not stolen — they are permanently frozen in the pool: unallocatedRewards only accrues while _totalSupply == 0, and this loss happens precisely while the pool is staked, so recoverUnallocatedRewards returns nothing for it. Stakers earn approximately zero while the UI shows a funded pool, and the creator cannot reclaim the difference. The same outcome is reachable with no attacker at all, by configuration alone, on a busy KUSDT-rewarded pool over a large 18-decimal LP.

Recommendation

Raise the accumulator precision so that a one-second settlement still produces a non-zero increment for any realistic decimals/stake combination. rewardRate and rewardPerTokenStored are now scaled by PRECISION = 1e36 and every step goes through Math.mulDiv, whose 512-bit intermediate makes the larger scale free of overflow risk. In the scenario above the one-second increment becomes 385 802 469 135 802 rather than 0.

notifyRewardAmount additionally rejects a schedule whose rate rounds to zero (require(rate > 0)). Carrying the remainder forward is the alternative fix if the precision change is ever considered too invasive; it was not needed here.

Recommended fix
uint256 public constant PRECISION = 1e36; function rewardPerToken() public view returns (uint256) {    return rewardPerTokenStored + Math.mulDiv(applicable - last, rewardRate, supply);} function earned(address account) public view returns (uint256) {    return Math.mulDiv(_balances[account],        rewardPerToken() - userRewardPerTokenPaid[account], PRECISION) + rewards[account];} function notifyRewardAmount(uint256 reward, ...) external {    uint256 rate = Math.mulDiv(reward, PRECISION, _rewardsDuration);    require(rate > 0, "Reward rate is zero");}

H-01 · Fee-on-transfer staking token desynchronises _totalSupply, stranding the last stakers' principal

Fixed

StakingRewards.sol:336-355 (`_credit`), StakingRewards.sol:299-307 (`withdraw`), StakingRewardsFactory.sol:134-199 (`deploy`)

Description

_credit records the requested amount into _totalSupply, _balances[msg.sender] and the deposit lot, and only afterwards does stake pull the tokens with safeTransferFrom. Nothing compares the balance before and after the transfer.

For a fee-on-transfer, deflationary or rebasing staking token the pool receives less than it recorded, and the gap compounds with every deposit. withdraw then pays out the recorded figure. Earlier withdrawers are paid in full out of later depositors' tokens, and the pool eventually holds less than _totalSupply claims.

The reward token is explicitly documented as not supporting fee-on-transfer, and the pool's balance check in notifyRewardAmount enforces that by reverting. The staking token has no equivalent guard, and deploy validates neither token — it only rejects a zero reward-token address.

Worked example with a 2% fee-on-transfer staking token: A stakes 100 (pool receives 98, _totalSupply = 100), B stakes 100 (pool receives 196 total, _totalSupply = 200). A withdraws 100 — the transfer succeeds, pool holds 96. B calls withdraw(100): the token's own balance check reverts. B's exit() reverts identically. B's principal is unreachable, and no admin path exists to make them whole.

Status: fixed. Regression test: test/Regressions.spec.ts. stake and stakeWithPermit now credit the balance delta measured across the transfer (_pull), so _totalSupply can never exceed the tokens the pool actually holds.

Vulnerable code
function _credit(uint256 amount) private {    require(amount > 0, "Cannot stake 0");     uint256 supply = _totalSupply + amount;    uint256 cap = maxStakingPower;    require(cap == 0 || supply <= cap, "Staking cap reached");    require(supply <= type(uint128).max, "Amount too large");     _totalSupply = uint128(supply);    _balances[msg.sender] += amount;    _deposits[msg.sender].push(        IStakingRewards.Deposit({amount: uint128(amount), ...})    );} function stake(uint256 amount) external nonReentrant updateReward(msg.sender) {    _credit(amount);    stakingToken.safeTransferFrom(msg.sender, address(this), amount);    emit Staked(msg.sender, amount);}
Impact

Permanent loss of principal for whichever stakers withdraw last, sized by the token's fee rate times the pool's deposit volume. Because pool creation is permissionless, this is also a deliberate rug vector: a creator can open a pool over a fee-on-transfer token, advertise rewards, and let the arithmetic trap depositors. Neither the creator nor the factory owner needs any privilege to reach the state, and there is no recovery function.

Recommendation

Record what the pool actually received, not what was asked for. Measure the staking-token balance before and after the pull and credit the difference — this also makes the deposit-lot amount, _balances and _totalSupply consistent by construction. Do the same in stakeWithPermit. If supporting such tokens is not intended, make that explicit instead: revert when received != amount so the pool fails loudly on the first deposit rather than silently on the last withdrawal.

Recommended fix
function stake(uint256 amount) external nonReentrant updateReward(msg.sender) {    uint256 before = stakingToken.balanceOf(address(this));    stakingToken.safeTransferFrom(msg.sender, address(this), amount);    uint256 received = stakingToken.balanceOf(address(this)) - before;    _credit(received);    emit Staked(msg.sender, received);} // or, if such tokens are simply out of scope, fail loudly on the way in:    require(received == amount, "Fee on transfer not supported");

H-03 · Every pool inherits its tokens' KAP-20 committee as a trusted party with full control of the pooled balance

Acknowledged

StakingRewardsFactory.sol:134-199 (`deploy`, no token validation), StakingRewards.sol:299-333 (`withdraw`, `getRewardFor`)

Description

The KAP-20 standard on KUB Chain adds privileged functions that have no ERC-20 equivalent, held by the token developer's committee and admin addresses:

  • adminTransfer(sender, recipient, amount) — the committee moves any holder's balance, including a contract's. The pool is a holder.
  • activateOnlyKycAddress() / setAcceptedKycLevel(level) — once enabled, transfers require the sender to hold an accepted KYC level. A pool contract holds none, so every safeTransfer out of it reverts.
  • pause() / addBlacklist(address) — the same freeze, reached a different way.

All three were confirmed present on KUB mainnet KUSDT (0x7d984C24d2499D840eB3b7016077164e15E5faA6), whose ABI exposes adminTransfer, activateOnlyKycAddress, setAcceptedKycLevel, addBlacklist and pause. That token is the most likely reward asset for pools on this factory.

deploy performs no validation on either token address — by design, since the factory is permissionless. Consequently every pool's principal and reward balance sit behind a third party's admin key that neither the protocol nor its users chose, with no on-chain recourse. This is expected on KUB rather than a coding error, but it is the largest single risk in the deployment and the report has to name it.

Status: acknowledged, accepted, and deliberately not mitigated in code. This is inherent to a permissionless factory over KAP-20 tokens and cannot be fixed in the pool.

An owner-controlled token allowlist was implemented and then removed on review, which is worth recording because it is the kind of control that looks like a fix and is not one:

  • It removes none of the risk it names. Listing a token does not take adminTransfer, addBlacklist, pause or activateOnlyKycAddress away from that token's committee; those powers remain in full after listing, and a token can be listed while benign and turned hostile afterwards.
  • It costs the thing the factory exists for. An allowlist gives the factory owner a veto over who may open a pool - a centralisation finding of its own, and a larger one than the risk it was meant to cover, since it applies to every creator on every token rather than to one token's committee.
  • It moves the trust rather than reducing it: users would read a listing as an endorsement the operator is not in a position to make.

The exposure is therefore handled where it can actually be handled - disclosure. The front end is expected to show each pool's token committee and its powers. Any deployment that genuinely wants curation should curate the *interface*, where a mistake is reversible, not the factory, where it is a permanent power.

Vulnerable code
function deploy(    address stakingToken,    address rewardsToken,    ...) public returns (address stakingRewards) {    require(rewardsToken != address(0), "StakingRewardsFactory: rewards token is zero");    require(rewardAmount > 0, "StakingRewardsFactory: reward amount is zero");    // stakingToken is not validated at all; neither token's admin powers are surfaced}
Impact

A malicious or compromised KAP-20 committee can drain a pool's entire staked principal and reward balance in one adminTransfer, or freeze every withdrawal permanently by enabling KYC-only transfers, blacklisting the pool, or pausing the token. Users have no recovery path in this codebase: withdraw, getReward and exit all terminate in a token transfer from the pool. Loss is the full TVL of every pool using that token.

Recommendation

Do not add a token allowlist to the factory; see the description for why it trades a real permission for an imaginary mitigation. Handle this off chain instead: show each pool's staking and reward token, its KAP-20 committee address and which of adminTransfer, addBlacklist, pause and activateOnlyKycAddress that token exposes, and let creators and stakers price the risk themselves. A curated front end list is the right place for curation, because it is reversible and it does not become a power over the protocol.

Recommended fix
// optional, governance-gated, default open so the permissionless property stays a choice:    mapping(address => bool) public tokenAllowed;    bool public allowlistEnabled;     function deploy(...) public returns (address stakingRewards) {        if (allowlistEnabled) {            require(tokenAllowed[stakingToken] && tokenAllowed[rewardsToken], "Token not allowed");        }        ...    } // and, regardless: disclose the committee of every listed token in the UI and the README.

H-04 · The reward accumulator can overflow, and it takes the withdrawal path with it - stakers' principal is frozen for good

Fixed

StakingRewards.sol:170-180 (`rewardPerToken`), StakingRewards.sol:567-586 (`updateReward`), StakingRewards.sol:488-520 (`notifyRewardAmount`)

Description

Found by asking directly whether withdraw can ever revert, rather than assuming it cannot.

rewardPerTokenStored is a monotonically increasing uint256 scaled by PRECISION = 1e36, advanced by Math.mulDiv(dt, rewardRate, totalStaked). The denominator is the staked supply, which may legitimately be as low as 1 wei. So one epoch's worst-case growth is reward * PRECISION, and nothing bounded it.

Once the accumulator overflows, Math.mulDiv reverts. That is not confined to the reward side: rewardPerToken() is called by the updateReward modifier, which runs on every stake, withdraw, withdrawFrom, getReward and exit. A pool in this state cannot be withdrawn from at all - the principal is trapped permanently, with no administrative path and no recovery.

The arithmetic, against type(uint256).max = 1.158e77:

`` budget 1e24 (1M 18-dec tokens), 1 token staked -> +1.0e42 per epoch (1.2e35 epochs of headroom) budget 1e24, 1 wei staked -> +1.0e60 per epoch (1.2e17 epochs) budget 1e33 (1e15 tokens), 1 wei staked -> +1.0e69 per epoch (1.2e08 epochs) budget 1e41 (1e23 tokens), 1 wei staked -> +1.0e77 per epoch (ONE epoch) ``

Reproduced: a 2e41 budget over a 1-day epoch against a 1 wei stake. Early in the epoch rewardPerToken() returns normally; by the epoch's end it reverts, and so does withdraw. The freeze arrives mid-epoch, so the pool works, accepts stakers, and then traps them - it is not a configuration that fails visibly at creation.

It does not need an attacker. A creator funding an ordinary farm on a token with an absurd supply reaches it by configuration alone, and the losers are that pool's stakers.

Rated High rather than Critical because a concrete precondition gates it: the budget must exceed roughly 1.16e41 base units, which needs a token with an extreme supply. The impact where it applies is total and permanent.

This headroom is 1e18 times smaller than it was before C-01: raising PRECISION from 1e18 to 1e36 is what bought the truncation fix, and it cost exactly this. The fix keeps both properties instead of trading one for the other.

Status: fixed. Regression tests: test/WithdrawLiveness.spec.ts.

Vulnerable code
function notifyRewardAmount(uint256 reward, ...) external {    // nothing bounded how far rewardPerTokenStored could grow    uint256 rate = Math.mulDiv(reward, PRECISION, _rewardsDuration);} function rewardPerToken() public view returns (uint256) {    // reverts once the sum exceeds uint256 - and updateReward calls this on every    // stake, withdraw and claim, so the principal goes with it    return rewardPerTokenStored + Math.mulDiv(applicable - last, rewardRate, supply);}
Impact

Every staker's principal in the affected pool is frozen permanently. Not stolen - unreachable, by anyone, including the creator, with no administrative override and no recovery path, because the settle that reverts runs before every balance change. Equivalent in effect to a total loss for that pool, and it can be reached by configuration alone with no attacker and no malice.

Recommendation

Bound the accumulator at configuration time, where the numbers are known, rather than hoping the staked supply stays large. notifyRewardAmount now caps a single epoch's budget at type(uint128).max (which makes reward * PRECISION always computable) and then requires that this epoch's worst-case growth still fits:

``solidity require(reward <= type(uint128).max, "Reward too large"); require( rewardPerTokenStored <= type(uint256).max - reward * PRECISION, "Reward exceeds accumulator headroom" ); ``

Because a whole epoch can grow the accumulator by at most reward * PRECISION (the supply divisor is at least 1), this check is exact and cumulative: it can never be exceeded afterwards, so rewardPerToken() can no longer revert, and therefore neither can the withdrawal path. A configuration that cannot be represented is now refused loudly at the moment it is set, instead of bricking withdrawals partway through an epoch.

earned() needs no separate bound: between settlements an account's balance is part of the supply the increment divides by, so an account's accrued amount is bounded by the epoch's budget.

Recommended fix
function notifyRewardAmount(uint256 reward, ...) external {    // one epoch can grow the accumulator by at most reward * PRECISION, because the    // supply divisor is at least 1. Check that in advance and it can never overflow.    require(reward <= type(uint128).max, "Reward too large");    require(        rewardPerTokenStored <= type(uint256).max - reward * PRECISION,        "Reward exceeds accumulator headroom"    );    uint256 rate = Math.mulDiv(reward, PRECISION, _rewardsDuration);}

M-01 · startEpoch never refreshes poolInfo, so the on-chain index reports the first epoch's schedule forever

Fixed

StakingRewardsFactory.sol:239-274 (`startEpoch`), StakingRewardsFactory.sol:37-50 (`StakingRewardsInfo`, `poolInfo`)

Description

deploy writes the full StakingRewardsInfo — rewardAmount, startTime, rewardsDuration, lockDuration, maxStakingPower, epoch: 1. startEpoch then increments info.epoch and passes the new parameters straight through to the pool, without writing any of them back to the struct.

From epoch 2 onwards poolInfo therefore describes a schedule that is no longer in force, while its epoch field says otherwise — a self-inconsistent record. A pool that ran epoch 1 with no lock and no cap, then opened epoch 2 with a 30-day lock and a cap, still reports lockDuration = 0 and maxStakingPower = 0 through poolInfo.

The README presents poolInfo as the on-chain index for exactly these fields, so an integrator following the documentation reads stale values. The live values are only available from the pool itself (lockDuration(), maxStakingPower(), periodFinish(), rewardsDuration()), or by replaying EpochStarted events.

Status: fixed. Regression test: test/Regressions.spec.ts. startEpoch now writes rewardAmount, startTime, rewardsDuration, lockDuration and maxStakingPower alongside the epoch number, so the index always describes the epoch that is running.

Vulnerable code
function startEpoch(address stakingRewards, uint256 rewardAmount, uint256 startTime, uint256 rewardsDuration, uint256 lockDuration, uint256 maxStakingPower) external {    StakingRewardsInfo storage info = poolInfo[stakingRewards];    require(msg.sender == info.creator, "StakingRewardsFactory: not the creator");    ...    uint256 epoch = info.epoch + 1;    info.epoch = epoch;    // startTime, rewardsDuration, lockDuration, maxStakingPower and rewardAmount    // in poolInfo still describe epoch 1    IERC20(info.rewardsToken).safeTransferFrom(msg.sender, stakingRewards, rewardAmount);}
Impact

A front end built on the documented index shows the wrong lock period, cap and budget from epoch 2 onwards. A user who stakes believing there is no lock discovers their tokens are locked for up to a year — the maximum epoch length — with no way to exit early, since there is deliberately no emergency withdrawal. No funds are stolen, but users take an irreversible action on information the contract itself supplied.

Recommendation

Write the new parameters into poolInfo in startEpoch, the same fields deploy sets. It is four assignments in a struct already loaded as storage. Alternatively, delete the duplicated schedule fields from StakingRewardsInfo and have integrators read the pool directly — that removes the divergence by construction and saves storage (see I-01). Do not leave both copies with only one of them maintained.

Recommended fix
    uint256 epoch = info.epoch + 1;    info.epoch = epoch;    info.rewardAmount = rewardAmount;    info.startTime = startTime;    info.rewardsDuration = rewardsDuration;    info.lockDuration = lockDuration;    info.maxStakingPower = maxStakingPower;    IERC20(info.rewardsToken).safeTransferFrom(msg.sender, stakingRewards, rewardAmount);
Medium

M-02 · Unbounded per-user deposit-lot array with no indexed withdrawal lets a staker permanently brick their own exit

Fixed

StakingRewards.sol:360-389 (`_debit`), StakingRewards.sol:234-259 (`withdrawableOf`, `nextUnlockAt`), StakingRewards.sol:327-333 (`exit`)

Description

Every stake pushes a new Deposit lot, and there is no cap on how many an account may hold. _debit walks that array from _withdrawCursor forward on every withdrawal, performing a storage read per lot and a storage write per lot it draws from, followed by a second while loop to advance the cursor. withdrawableOf and nextUnlockAt walk the same array, and exit() walks it twice — once through withdrawableOf and again through _debit.

The cursor only advances past a drained prefix. A user whose oldest lots are still locked keeps re-scanning them on every withdrawal, so the cost does not amortise while any early lot remains locked. Crucially, withdraw takes an amount, not an index: there is no way to reach a specific unlocked lot without scanning everything before it.

On KUB, where gas is cheap, creating several thousand dust stakes is inexpensive.

Only the account itself can grow its own array — _credit uses msg.sender — so this cannot be inflicted on a victim. It remains a permanent, unrecoverable freeze of that account's principal, and there is no emergency path around it.

Fixed

Description

L-03 · unchecked blocks in the accumulator rely on bounds that are never stated or enforced

Fixed

Description

I-05 · A broken reward token stops exit() but not withdraw() - by design, and worth telling stakers

Acknowledged

StakingRewards.sol:328-337 (`withdraw`), StakingRewards.sol:379-383 (`exit`), StakingRewards.sol:397-405 (`_claim`)

Description

Checked while establishing what can block a withdrawal. exit() withdraws and claims, so it transfers the reward token. If that token is paused, blacklists the pool, or is switched to KYC-only transfers - all of which a KAP-20 committee may do at any time - exit() reverts in full, including the part that would have returned the principal.

Combined with the H-04 fix, which guarantees the accrual settle cannot revert either, the principal always has a way out that does not depend on the reward side at all. This is the correct arrangement - a claim that cannot be paid must not silently succeed, and a claim failing must not hold the principal hostage. It is filed as Informational because it is a property stakers and front ends need to know rather than a defect: a UI that only offers exit() would present a broken reward token as a frozen pool.

Impact

No loss. A front end that offers only exit() would leave stakers believing their principal is stuck when it is not.

Recommendation

Keep the separation and document it, which the NatSpec on withdraw now does. Analysis

Method & verification

Reviewed across the five dimensions in the methodology: access control, core protocol logic (the epoch state machine), value accounting, low-level Solidity safety, and reward-accumulator mathematics. Every in-scope file was read end to end; nothing was sampled.

The five mandatory passes were all performed: arithmetic (every narrowing cast, every denominator, every unchecked block, the monotonic accumulator against its type max), liveness (the seven positive checks, answered in the section below), loops and gas, centralization, and value accounting.

Each candidate was re-checked with the goal of refuting it — accounting for checked arithmetic in 0.8.19, the nonReentrant guards, the require further up the call path, and whether the assumed caller can actually reach the state. Nine candidates were dropped this way, including: reward over-promise via notifyRewardAmount (the factory always notifies exactly what it transferred in the same call), cross-pool reward theft (pools share no balance), getRewardFor payout redirection (it pays the account, never the caller), exit() nested-reentrancy (the two guarded calls are sequential, not nested), and monotonic overflow of rewardPerTokenStored (~1e33 epochs at the worst realistic rate). 12 verified findings, 12 distinct after de-duplication.

Tooling: scripts/scan.py recon, forge build --sizes, and the project's own 46-test suite (npx mocha, all passing) used to confirm behaviour rather than to find it. Slither was not available in this environment and was not run — a static pass should be added before deployment.

Produced with model: opus, effort: high, solc 0.8.19, forge 1.6.0-nightly, against the working tree at commit c580963.

4Is every denominator provably non-zero?Yes — rewardPerToken divides by _totalSupply only inside supply != 0; notifyRewardAmount divides by _rewardsDuration after require(> 0); every other division is by the constant 1e18.
5Does every narrowing cast use SafeCast or have a proven bound?Almost — uint128(amount), uint128(remaining), uint32(_rewardsDuration) and uint40(block.timestamp) are all bounded by a preceding require. uint40(begin + _rewardsDuration) is not (L-01).
6Does the monotonic accumulator fit its type for the contract's lifetime?Yes — rewardPerTokenStored grows by at most reward * 1e18 per epoch. At a 1e26 budget against a 1-wei stake, that is ~1e44 per epoch; ~1e33 epochs would be needed to reach uint256 max.
7Is there an emergency withdrawal that skips reward accounting?No, and it is not needed: updateReward performs no external call and cannot revert on anything an attacker controls. Noted as I-02 rather than filed as a gap.

C-01 quantified — truncation loss per settlement

The accumulator advances by floor((Δt × rewardRate) / totalStaked). The remainder is discarded, but lastUpdateTime advances regardless, so whoever picks Δt picks how much of the budget survives. getRewardFor(address) takes an arbitrary account and has no access control, so Δt is attacker-controlled and can be one block.

Budget 1 000 KUSDT = 1e9 base units over 30 days, against 1 000 000 units of an 18-decimal LP = 1e24 staked, at the audited PRECISION = 1e18:

``` rewardRate = 1e9 × 1e18 / 2 592 000 = 385802469135802469135

Δt increment per settlement 1s 0 5s 0 60s 0 3600s 1 2592000s 999

settled every block (518 400 settlements) -> rewardPerToken total = 0 settled once, at the end of the epoch -> rewardPerToken total = 999 ```

So the pool pays 999 000 000 of its 1 000 000 000 base units when left alone, and 0 when ground down one block at a time. The stranded remainder is unrecoverable: unallocatedRewards only accrues while _totalSupply == 0, and this loss occurs while the pool is staked.

With PRECISION = 1e36 the one-second increment becomes 385 802 469 135 802 — non-zero at every Δt, so the attack has nothing to grind against. Math.mulDiv keeps the 512-bit intermediate, so the larger scale costs no headroom.

Fork diff — what changed from upstream

Lineage: Synthetix StakingRewards v2.27.2 → Uniswap liquidity-staker → this repo. Diffed against both. Deviations are deliberate and documented in NatSpec; these are the ones that change security properties.

  • rewardRate scaled by 1e18 — numerically equivalent to upstream's Δt × rate × 1e18 / supply, and strictly better on the rate division. It does not remove the final truncation against totalStaked (H-02), so the README's claim that precision is fixed is only half true.
  • Per-deposit lock lots replace upstream's single per-user record — new code, new unbounded loop (M-02).
  • notifyRewardAmount one-shot per epoch, gated on block.timestamp >= periodFinish — this removes upstream's mid-period top-up and with it the classic "claim front-run by a rate change" issue. A live epoch cannot be re-rated. Verified.
  • getRewardFor(account) is permissionless — safe because the payout goes to account, never to msg.sender. Verified against the batch-claim path.
  • stakeWithPermit retained from Uniswap — inert for KAP-20 tokens, which have no permit. No risk, but it is dead weight in the bytecode on this chain.
  • The factory holds no balance and no genesis gate — upstream's factory custodied every pool's rewards until notifyRewardAmounts. Removing that removes a shared honeypot and the never-started stranding case. Verified.

No constant, comparison operator or rounding direction was found altered from upstream in a way that changes who gets paid — the failure mode of the Uranium-class fork bug.
